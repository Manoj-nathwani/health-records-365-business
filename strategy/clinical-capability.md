# Clinical capability

## The boundary is a choice, not a prohibition

[The regulatory boundary](../trust/regulatory-boundary.md) says we do not diagnose, recommend treatment or decide which tests someone needs. That reads like a legal constraint. It is not.

**A GMC-registered clinician can do all of it.** Private GP services do it every day. The reason we do not is that we are not registered to, and becoming registered is a decision with consequences rather than a feature to add.

So the question is not *are we allowed* but *what does it cost, and what does it buy*.

## Why it is worth asking

**We stop at the moment of maximum value.** Someone has just been shown five unresolved items in their own record. Their next sentence is *"so what do I do?"* and the answer is *"find a doctor"* — a handoff at the exact point of greatest trust and greatest need.

**The open-loop lifecycle does not actually close.** [Our differentiation](../market/differentiation.md) claims we track unresolved threads until their status changes. We can observe a loop; we cannot close one. Closing requires somebody to order the test or make the referral, and that is a third party we do not control.

**The [reference implementation](../operations/reference-implementation.md) demonstrates the gap.** A record identified exactly what needed doing; the GP declined three test requests in two weeks; the answer was to fly to Asia and buy them privately. The record worked and nothing could act on it.

**It is where the money is.** [SIP](../market/competitors/sip-medical-family-office.md) charges thousands a year, and physician review is part of why. Record management alone has a price ceiling; the [premium tier](../commercial/pricing.md) probably requires clinical involvement to justify itself.

## The ladder

This is not binary. Five distinct positions, in increasing order of regulatory weight.

| | What it is | Regulatory cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **0. State the record** | What happened, what is unresolved, nothing more | None. Where we are |
| **1. Prepare the question** | The three things to raise with a clinician, and the evidence behind each | Effectively none |
| **2. Navigate** | Find the provider, compare prices, book it, get the outcome back into the record | None. Concierge, not clinical |
| **3. Bought-in clinical review** | A registered clinician reviews the completed record and writes an opinion, under their own name and indemnity | Carried by the clinician |
| **4. Partnered clinical service** | Refer to a registered provider who can order, prescribe and follow up. Revenue share | Carried by the partner |
| **5. Own clinical arm** | Employ or contract clinicians; become a healthcare provider | Full. A different company |

## Where to stand

**Steps 1–2 are the core product, not an add-on.** Question preparation, provider research, itinerary planning and booking are [health navigation](../product/navigation.md) — the independent planner over the record, and the answer to *"so what do I do?"* that needs no registration at all. It also produces the outcome documents that feed back into the record, which is the [retention loop](../commercial/retention-loop.md) working as designed.

**Step 3–4 as the deliberate expansion**, once there are customers. Step 4 is probably the sweet spot: the customer's loop actually closes, the partner holds the registration and the indemnity, and we keep the thing that makes us useful. **The concrete design is [the record review](../product/record-review.md)** — a tele-GP consultation over the completed record.

**Step 5 only with a reason that does not currently exist.**

## What step 5 actually costs

Not a feature. A regulated healthcare business: clinician registration, clinical governance, indemnity, complaints handling, safeguarding, prescribing controls, and — in England, for regulated activities — CQC registration.

### The cross-border problem is the hard one

**Our customer is abroad by definition.** A UK-registered GP advising a patient physically in Indonesia raises questions with no comfortable answers: where is care being delivered, which regulator has jurisdiction, and does UK indemnity cover it? Most does not.

This is not a general objection to telemedicine. It is specific to us, because [the target customer](../market/target-customer.md) is selected *for* being outside the UK. **The segment that makes the records product attractive is the segment that makes the clinical product hardest.**

### Liability changes shape

Missing a record is a service failure. Missing a diagnosis is a different category, and it is not insurable at startup scale — see [liability](../legal/liability.md).

### It complicates the partner channel

An adviser introducing a client to a records service is a low-risk act. Introducing them to a medical service is a different calculation and may hit the adviser's own compliance rules. [Sojourner's panel](../go-to-market/sojourners-capital.md) is easier to join as a records firm.

## The trap to avoid entirely

**Having the software suggest tests.** It is the tempting shortcut — no clinician to hire, no registration to obtain.

It is the worst available option. Software that interprets patient-specific data and recommends clinical action can itself fall within medical-device regulation, determined by intended purpose. That is regulatory exposure **without** a clinician's cover, and it breaks [the AI position](../product/ai-positioning.md) at the same time.

If clinical judgement is ever offered, it comes from a named registered human who owns it.

## The incentive problem, if we ever sell both

Part of why the record is trustworthy is that we have no stake in what it says. A service that both surfaces unresolved items and sells the follow-up has an obvious conflict, and *"we found five things, and our doctor can help with all five"* reads very differently from a neutral finding.

**If steps 3–5 ever happen, the firewall has to be explicit and public**: whoever maintains the record has no financial interest in what downstream care is recommended. Otherwise the open-items list becomes a sales funnel and the [trust position](../trust/data-sovereignty.md) is spent.

## The sequencing argument

The order matters more than the destination.

A private GP service is a commodity — many exist, and they compete on price and convenience. **A GP holding a complete, reconciled thirty-year history is not a commodity**, because almost no clinician anywhere has ever had that.

So the record is the asset that would make a clinical service differentiated, and building it first is correct. Building the clinical arm first would mean competing on somebody else's terms with none of the advantage.

> **The record is the moat. Clinical capability is a way to monetise it — in that order, never the reverse.**

## What would move us up the ladder

- Customers repeatedly asking *"so what do I do?"* and going nowhere with the answer
- Open items failing to close because nobody will act on them, making [the lifecycle claim](../product/open-loops.md) hollow
- The [premium tier](../commercial/pricing.md) failing to sell without clinical content
- A partner appearing who already holds the registration, indemnity and cross-border position

The last one is worth watching for actively. It converts a company-changing decision into a commercial agreement.
